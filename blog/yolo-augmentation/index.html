Data Augmentation in YOLO: What the Model Actually Sees

A walk through the training augmentations Ultralytics YOLO applies by default (mosaic, random scale and shift, HSV jitter, flips) and the ones it leaves off (rotation, shear, MixUp, CutMix), using the library's own code, interactive re-implementations in the browser, measured statistics, and a training ablation that adds them one at a time.

Key takeaways

  1. Ultralytics trains on images generated on the fly: four photos stitched into a mosaic, rescaled 0.5–1.5×, shifted, color-jittered and mirrored, with the boxes moved along. With the defaults, a training image of the African Wildlife dataset holds 4.25 objects instead of 1.84, and 61 % of them touch the image edge, most because they were cut off.
  2. Geometric augmentation has to transform the boxes too, and that is where it can hurt. Boxes are recomputed from their transformed corners, so a 45° rotation gives a round object a training box with IoU 0.5 against its real extent. That is why rotation and shear are off by default when labels are boxes.
  3. Fine-tuning a COCO-pretrained YOLO26n on 200 photos, the defaults raised mAP50-95 from 0.613 to 0.701 (mean of three seeds). Horizontal flips and random scale and shift did most of it; HSV, mosaic on top, MixUp and keeping mosaic to the end were all within seed-to-seed noise.
  4. Trained from scratch on 1,052 photos, the model without augmentation memorized its training set (mAP50-95 0.256); the defaults without mosaic reached 0.562, and with mosaic 0.633. Choose augmentations by asking which changes leave your labels true, and look at the training batches.

A YOLO model trained with Ultralytics almost never sees a photo from its training set. It sees something like this:

Every sample is stitched together from four photos, rescaled, shifted, recolored and sometimes mirrored, and the boxes are moved along with the pixels. None of these images exists on disk. They are generated on the fly, so in 100 epochs the model sees 100 different versions of every photo.

This is data augmentation: random changes to a training image that keep its labels valid. A zebra mirrored left to right is still a zebra, and a slightly darker photo of a buffalo still shows a buffalo. Augmentation makes a small dataset behave like a larger one, and it teaches the model which changes should not matter.

This post goes through the augmentations Ultralytics applies to detection training, one at a time: what each does, how it is implemented, and why the defaults are what they are. For the figures, I reimplemented the pipeline in JavaScript and checked it against the Python code (more on that below), so everything you can click on runs the same math as the library. At the end, I train YOLO26n with the augmentations switched on one at a time to measure what each is worth.

The examples use the African Wildlife dataset that Ultralytics publishes: 1,052 training and 225 validation photos of buffalo, elephants, rhinos and zebras. All code references are to Ultralytics 8.4.46.

The default recipe

These are the augmentation settings in ultralytics/cfg/default.yaml, which every detection training run starts from:

Setting Default What it does
mosaic 1.0 Combine four images into one (probability)
close_mosaic 10 Turn mosaic off for the last 10 epochs
scale 0.5 Random zoom between 0.5× and 1.5×
translate 0.1 Random shift of up to ±10 % of the image size
fliplr 0.5 Mirror left to right (probability)
hsv_h 0.015 Random hue shift of up to ±1.5 % of the color circle
hsv_s 0.7 Random saturation gain between 0.3× and 1.7×
hsv_v 0.4 Random brightness gain between 0.6× and 1.4×
degrees, shear, perspective 0 Rotation, shear, perspective: off
flipud 0.0 Vertical flip: off
mixup, cutmix, copy_paste 0.0 Mixing two images: off
bgr 0.0 Swap the red and blue channels: off

They are put together in one function, v8_transforms in ultralytics/data/augment.py. Stripped of the keypoint and copy-paste handling, it reads:

python
mosaic = Mosaic(dataset, imgsz=imgsz, p=hyp.mosaic)
affine = RandomPerspective(
    degrees=hyp.degrees,
    translate=hyp.translate,
    scale=hyp.scale,
    shear=hyp.shear,
    perspective=hyp.perspective,
    pre_transform=None if stretch else LetterBox(new_shape=(imgsz, imgsz)),
)

pre_transform = Compose([mosaic, affine])

return Compose(
    [
        pre_transform,
        MixUp(dataset, pre_transform=pre_transform, p=hyp.mixup),
        CutMix(dataset, pre_transform=pre_transform, p=hyp.cutmix),
        Albumentations(p=1.0, transforms=getattr(hyp, "augmentations", None)),
        RandomHSV(hgain=hyp.hsv_h, sgain=hyp.hsv_s, vgain=hyp.hsv_v),
        RandomFlip(direction="vertical", p=hyp.flipud, flip_idx=flip_idx),
        RandomFlip(direction="horizontal", p=hyp.fliplr, flip_idx=flip_idx),
    ]
)  # transforms

The order matters. Geometry comes first, because it decides which objects end up in the image and where. Mixing with a second image comes next, then the changes that only touch pixel values, and the flips last. Validation images get none of this: they are only resized and padded to 640 × 640 (LetterBox), so that the model is evaluated on the photos as they are.

How the browser version was checked. I recorded every random number the Python classes drew for 22 test cases (single images with every geometric option switched on, mosaics, color changes), fed the same numbers to the JavaScript code, and compared the results. Box coordinates agree to within 10−4 px and the same boxes are kept and dropped. The mosaic canvas is pixel-identical. Resampled pixels differ by 0.03–0.27 gray levels on average (at most 6), because OpenCV interpolates in fixed-point arithmetic; colors after the HSV step differ by 0.3 levels on average (at most 2).

Mosaic

Mosaic was introduced with YOLOv4 (Bochkovskiy et al., 2020) and has been on by default in Ultralytics YOLO ever since. It takes the requested image, adds three more, and tiles them around a random point on a canvas twice the training size:

python
s = self.imgsz
yc, xc = (int(random.uniform(-x, 2 * s + x)) for x in self.border)  # mosaic center x, y
for i in range(4):
    labels_patch = labels if i == 0 else labels["mix_labels"][i - 1]

With imgsz = 640, the border is −320, so the center lands anywhere in the middle half of the 1280 × 1280 canvas, and the four images are cut wherever they cross the canvas edge. The other three images are not drawn from the whole dataset but from a buffer of recently loaded images (up to eight batches’ worth), which keeps the data loader from reading four files for every sample.

The canvas is then passed to RandomPerspective with that border of −320, which means its output is 640 × 640: a window of the mosaic, zoomed by the random scale and shifted by the random translation. That window is what Figure 2 draws as a dashed square.

What does this do to the training data? I ran the real data loader over the training set three times (3,156 samples) with three settings and counted:

Per training sample No augmentation Defaults without mosaic Defaults
Objects (mean) 1.84 1.82 4.25
Median object size 235 px 211 px 159 px
Objects touching the image edge 8.8 % 22.8 % 60.7 %

Mosaic more than doubles the number of objects per image and makes them smaller. In this dataset, where most photos show one or two large animals, that is a big change: the model sees animals at more sizes, in more surroundings, and next to other species, and every batch effectively contains four times as many photos. The YOLOv4 authors also point out that batch normalization then computes its statistics over four times as many images per batch.

The third row is the price. Six in ten objects now touch the edge of the training image, almost always because the window or the canvas cut them off (in the original photos, fewer than one in ten do), and many images have gray areas and hard seams where one photo ends and the next begins. Real photos don’t look like that. This is why Ultralytics turns mosaic off for the last 10 epochs (close_mosaic=10): the model finishes training on images that look like the ones it will be tested on. (Whether that helps is measured below; in my runs it did not.) Scale, shift, rotation: RandomPerspective

All geometric augmentation happens in one class, RandomPerspective. It draws eight random numbers and builds one 3 × 3 matrix:

python
# Combined rotation matrix
M = T @ S @ R @ P @ C  # order of operations (right to left) is IMPORTANT

Read from right to left: move the image center to the origin (C), apply a perspective distortion (P), rotate and scale (R), shear (S), and move the result to a random position near the center of the output (T). The image is resampled through this matrix with cv2.warpAffine (or cv2.warpPerspective if perspective is on), and the empty area is filled with gray 114.

Boxes cannot simply be resampled. Ultralytics transforms the four corners of each box and takes the axis-aligned rectangle around them. Then it throws away boxes that no longer make sense:

python
return (w2 > wh_thr) & (h2 > wh_thr) & (w2 * h2 / (w1 * h1 + eps) > area_thr) & (ar < ar_thr)  # candidates

A box survives if it is wider and taller than 2 px, keeps more than 10 % of its (scaled) original area, and has an aspect ratio below 100. An animal that has been pushed almost entirely out of the frame is therefore removed from the labels rather than kept as a sliver.

With the defaults, only scale (0.5–1.5×) and translation (±10 %) are on. Try the others:

Why rotation is off by default

Rotation looks harmless, but it damages the labels. A box only says “the object is somewhere in this rectangle”. After rotating, the rectangle becomes a tilted rectangle, and the new axis-aligned box has to enclose its corners, which are usually background:

How much too big? That depends on the shape of the object, which a box does not record. For an object that fills its box like an ellipse (a rough stand-in for an animal), the geometry can be worked out exactly:

The model is then trained to predict those inflated boxes, and evaluated with mAP50-95, which demands IoU up to 0.95. Even degrees=10 produces training boxes with IoU of about 0.75 for round objects at the extremes of its range. With segmentation labels the problem disappears: RandomPerspective transforms the polygon instead and recomputes a tight box from it. This is why rotation is off for detection by default, and why it is much safer for segmentation datasets and for roughly rectangular objects.

Color: RandomHSV

After the geometry, RandomHSV changes the colors. It converts the image to OpenCV’s HSV representation (hue 0–179, saturation and value 0–255), draws three random gains, and applies them with lookup tables:

python
r = np.random.uniform(-1, 1, 3) * [self.hgain, self.sgain, self.vgain]  # random gains
x = np.arange(0, 256, dtype=r.dtype)
# lut_hue = ((x * (r[0] + 1)) % 180).astype(dtype)   # original hue implementation from ultralytics<=8.3.78
lut_hue = ((x + r[0] * 180) % 180).astype(dtype)
lut_sat = np.clip(x * (r[1] + 1), 0, 255).astype(dtype)
lut_val = np.clip(x * (r[2] + 1), 0, 255).astype(dtype)
lut_sat[0] = 0  # prevent pure white changing color, introduced in 8.3.79

Hue is shifted, which rotates every color around the color circle by the same amount; saturation and brightness are multiplied. The defaults are very different in size:

Saturation and brightness vary a lot, because lighting and cameras vary a lot: overcast days, dusk, a washed-out phone camera. Hue barely moves (±5.4° on the color circle). A larger hue range would turn the red dirt road green or the grass blue, and for classes that differ mainly by color (ripe and unripe fruit, traffic lights, colored parts), it would destroy the very information the model needs.

Two more pixel-level augmentations exist but are off in a default setup. bgr swaps the red and blue channels with the given probability. And if the Albumentations library is installed, Ultralytics adds blur, median blur, grayscale and CLAHE contrast enhancement, each with probability 1 %; it is not installed with Ultralytics by default (only with the extra dependencies), and it was not installed for anything in this post.

Flips

The last two steps mirror the image: left to right with probability 0.5, top to bottom never (flipud=0). Photos of the world have a direction: the sky is up and objects stand on the ground, but left and right are equivalent. A zebra facing left is as common as one facing right.

For keypoints, flipping also has to swap left and right labels (the left eye becomes the right eye), which is what the flip_idx list in a pose dataset’s YAML is for. Without it, Ultralytics turns flips off for pose training.

Mixing two images: MixUp, CutMix, copy-paste

Three more augmentations combine a training image with a second one. All are off by default for detection.

  • MixUp (Zhang et al., 2018) blends two complete training samples (each already a mosaic) with a ratio drawn from a Beta(32, 32) distribution, so almost always close to 50 : 50. Unlike in classification, the labels are not weighted: both images’ boxes are kept in full, and the model has to find every animal in a double exposure.
  • CutMix (Yun et al., 2019) pastes a rectangle from the second image. The Ultralytics version picks the rectangle among three random candidates that do not overlap any existing box, so it never covers an object of the first image, and keeps the second image’s boxes that have at least 10 % of their area inside the rectangle.
  • Copy-paste (Ghiasi et al., 2021) cuts out objects along their segmentation masks and pastes them elsewhere. It needs polygon labels, so it does nothing on a box-only dataset like this one.

The Ultralytics guide describes MixUp as a way to improve robustness and reduce overfitting. Whether that pays off depends on how much a model overfits in the first place, which the next section tests.

What is each augmentation worth?

To measure that, I trained YOLO26n with the augmentations switched on one at a time, in two settings that bracket most real projects. All runs used Ultralytics 8.4.46 on an RTX 3080 Ti, with every other training setting at its default (640 px images, batch 16, the default optimizer and learning-rate schedule), and were evaluated on the 225 validation photos, which are never augmented.

Fine-tuning on 200 photos

The common case is a small dataset and a model pretrained on COCO. I fine-tuned YOLO26n from its COCO weights for 50 epochs on 200 randomly chosen training photos, and trained every configuration three times with different random seeds.

Together, the defaults raise mAP50-95 from 0.613 to 0.701 and mAP50 from 0.757 to 0.858. Most of that comes from two augmentations: horizontal flips (+0.031) and random scale and shift (+0.041). HSV (+0.008), and mosaic on top of everything else (+0.008), add less than the spread between seeds, which reaches 0.046 for the same configuration (0.596 to 0.642 without augmentation). This experiment cannot tell whether they help here.

The two changes to the defaults made no measurable difference either. MixUp gave 0.696 against 0.701. Keeping mosaic until the end (close_mosaic=0) gave 0.709: slightly better than the default, not worse. You can see why in the training curves. When mosaic switches off after epoch 40, the default runs first lose a little (0.686 to 0.672 at epoch 42) while they adapt to whole images, and they have not quite caught up 8 epochs later:

Training from scratch on 1,052 photos

Without COCO pretraining, the model has to learn everything from this dataset, and augmentation becomes its main defense against memorizing it. I trained YOLO26n from random weights on the full training set for 100 epochs, once per configuration (one seed only, so small differences should not be over-read).

Without augmentation, the model overfits badly. Its validation classification loss is lowest at epoch 28 (1.80) and then climbs to 2.71 by epoch 100, while its training loss falls to 0.11: it is memorizing the training photos. It ends at mAP50-95 0.256. The defaults without mosaic reach 0.562, and with mosaic 0.633. Here mosaic is worth +0.07, nine times as much as in the fine-tuning experiment.

So the value of each augmentation depends on how much the model has to learn from your data. Starting from COCO weights with a few hundred photos, flips and random scale and shift did most of the work. Training from scratch, everything counted, mosaic included. In neither setting did any change I tried beat the defaults by more than the noise.

Choosing augmentations for your data

The defaults are tuned for photos of everyday scenes, like COCO. Whether they fit your data is a question about which changes leave your labels true:

  • Is there an up and down? For images taken from above (aerial photos, microscopy, a camera over a conveyor belt), orientation carries no meaning. Turn on flipud=0.5, and consider rotation, keeping the box problem above in mind.
  • Is there a left and right? Text, digits, arrows and road signs change meaning when mirrored, and so do classes like “left hand” and “right hand”. Set fliplr=0.
  • Does color define a class? Then keep hsv_h small or zero, and consider lowering hsv_s.
  • Do object sizes vary? With a fixed camera at a fixed distance, objects always have about the same size, and a 0.5–1.5× scale range trains for sizes that never occur. A smaller scale may help; measure it.
  • Are objects already tiny? Mosaic and down-scaling make them smaller still. Check how many boxes the size filter drops, and how small the remaining ones get.
  • How much does the model have to learn? In the experiments above, augmentation mattered most when training from scratch and much less when fine-tuning pretrained weights, where only flips and random scale and shift made a clear difference.

And whatever you change, look at the result. Ultralytics saves the first training batches as train_batch0.jpg to train_batch2.jpg in the run folder. They are the quickest way to catch a label that no longer matches its image, and sometimes one that never did: the first frame of the video in Figure 4 contains a training photo (4 (376).jpg) whose three zebras are all labeled as buffalo. A scan of the training and validation sets with a COCO-pretrained model, which knows zebras, found no other image with confidently detected zebras but no zebra label.

Cheat sheet

Setting Default Turn up when Turn down or off when
mosaic 1.0 Small dataset, few objects per image Objects are tiny; image context matters
